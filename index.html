A Deep Dive into OCTG History

The Connections That Became an Industry Standard

How a 1933 startup in Torrance, California built the premium thread that the entire oil and gas world still runs on

PH6 • CS • Hydril • 1933 – Present

Chapter I

A Company Born in the Depression

In 1933, with the Great Depression tightening its grip on America, a man named Frank Roger Seaver made what must have seemed like a reckless bet. He took his life savings and purchased the struggling Doheny Stone Drill Company—a small outfit in Torrance, California with 50 to 75 employees, fighting for survival in an oil industry that was itself barely hanging on.

Seaver renamed the company Hydril—a combination of "hydraulic drilling equipment"—and made a pivotal decision. He dropped the company's general drilling equipment line to focus exclusively on specialty items. It was a bet-the-company move, and his employees, grateful to have jobs during uncertain times, threw themselves into making it work.

Within two years, they turned the company profitable.

Seaver operated as a "benevolent dictator who compartmentalized information and personally controlled all major decisions." His philosophy was built on two words: simplicity and quality.

— Encyclopedia.com, Hydril Company Profile

By the late 1930s, Hydril had produced the first hydraulically operated blowout preventer—a device so durable it actually hurt replacement sales. Seaver's solution was characteristically clever: he pivoted to a leasing model "to avoid saturating the market." In 1937, the company expanded with a manufacturing plant in Rochester, Pennsylvania.

In 1946, an engineer named Granville Sloan Knox invented the spherical annular blowout preventer at Hydril. The annular BOP became so synonymous with the company that to this day, field hands often call any annular preventer simply "the Hydril"—the ultimate tribute to a brand that became the generic name for its category.

Chapter II

The PH6: A Thread That Changed Everything

PH stands for "Premium Hydril." The 6 means six threads per inch. It sounds unremarkable on paper. In practice, the PH6 became one of the most consequential pieces of engineering in the history of oil and gas.

The design that would become PH6 emerged from Hydril's pioneering work on two-step, non-interference thread profiles—a concept that was in commercial use by the mid-1970s. By the time the first patents describing "Hydril-type" connections appeared in 1976, the two-step thread was already established technology across the industry.

What Made It Revolutionary

The PH6's genius was its two-step, non-interference thread form. Threads are machined on two different diameters—two "steps"—providing extremely deep, stable stabbing. The non-interference design means the pin threads never contact the box threads during entry, which virtually eliminates the cross-threading that plagued earlier connections.

The seal system uses three independent metal-to-metal contact surfaces: a 30-degree reverse-angle external torque shoulder, a 90-degree intermediate shoulder, and a pin nose/box bore contact. No rubber. No elastomers. Just precision-machined steel sealing on steel, delivering gas-tight integrity at pressures exceeding 20,000 PSI.

The result was a connection that was fast to run, nearly impossible to cross-thread, gas-tight under extreme conditions, reusable across multiple make-and-break cycles, and proven in corrosive-resistant alloy (CRA) applications where galling is the constant enemy. It was, by every measure, a premium connection that earned its name.

Trouble-free make-up is routine with Hydril's two-step, non-interference thread style. The design combines the reusability of non-interference threads, metal seals, and substantial torque shoulders.

— TenarisHydril Running Manual, GDL23346

Chapter III

The CS: Compression Seal, Different Purpose

While PH6 conquered the workstring market, Hydril's engineers were building something else entirely. The CS—Compression Seal—was designed for a different set of demands: casing, completion strings, and the punishing world of macaroni tubing.

The CS was born from US Patent 4,521,042, filed July 5, 1983, by Hydril engineers Jan W. Blackburn and Burl E. Baron. The patent describes the innovation that would define the CS: two-stepped hooked threads with dissimilar-angled positive torque shoulders, creating a connection with dual torque shoulders—both an external and an intermediate—rather than PH6's single external shoulder arrangement.

This dual-shoulder design gives the CS up to 50% superior compression strength compared to single-shoulder connections. The three-point seal system—external, intermediate, and internal, all metal-to-metal—earned the CS a CAL IV gas-tight certification, the highest qualification level under ISO 13679.

Together, the PH6 and CS covered virtually every downhole application an operator could encounter. PH6 dominated workstrings and production tubing. CS owned casing, completions, and the small-diameter macaroni tubing market where few premium alternatives existed. Both shared the same running manual, the same field crews, and the same reputation for reliability that had been building since 1933.

Chapter IV

Patent protection is the invisible architecture beneath every premium connection. Understanding when those patents were filed—and when they expired—is the key to understanding why the market looks the way it does today.

The critical window: between 1995 and 2005, every foundational patent covering the Hydril two-step thread form, CS connection design, and wedge thread technology expired. The technology that had been protected for nearly two decades was now in the public domain.

Patent expiration did not end all legal disputes. Hydril—and later Tenaris—fought to protect improvements, particularly the Wedge Thread technology. The most significant battle, Hydril v. Grant Prideco (2003–2007), reached the Federal Circuit over wedge thread IP and antitrust claims. But notably, the major litigation centered on next-generation designs, not PH6 or CS. By the time Hydril was aggressively litigating, the foundational Legacy Series patents had already entered the public domain.

Chapter V

The $2.16 Billion Bet

$2.16B
Tenaris Acquisition of Hydril • May 7, 2007

On February 12, 2007, Luxembourg-based steel giant Tenaris S.A. announced it would acquire Hydril Company at $97 per share—a 30% premium over the 30-day average stock price. The deal closed on May 7, 2007, for approximately $2.16 billion in equity value.

At the time, Hydril operated 13 manufacturing plants worldwide, with 2006 revenue of $503 million and net income of $91.3 million. The Seaver family—in its third generation under Christopher T. Seaver—had retained majority ownership.

The acquisition would enable the combined company to offer customers worldwide a full range of integral and coupled premium connection products for the most demanding applications.

— Paolo Rocca, Tenaris Chairman and CEO, February 2007

The premium connections business was retained and branded TenarisHydril. In April 2008, Tenaris sold Hydril's pressure control division to General Electric for approximately $1.115 billion—meaning Tenaris effectively paid just over $1 billion for the premium connections business alone.

Three generations of the Seaver family had built Hydril from a 50-person machine shop into a global brand. Now its connections—PH6, CS, and PH4—would be classified as the "Legacy Series" within the TenarisHydril portfolio.

Chapter VI

The Market Today

When the foundational patents expired between 1995 and 2005, the door opened—and an entire industry walked through it. The PH6 thread form had become so dominant, so embedded in operator specifications and field crew muscle memory, that it achieved something rare for a proprietary design: it became a de facto industry standard. Not through a standards body. Through sheer market dominance.

TenarisHydril classifies PH6, PH4, and CS as its Legacy Series—but the label has not slowed demand. These connections are still manufactured at the premium threading facility in Westwego, Louisiana. When operators write “PH6-type” on a work order today, they might receive product from any of a dozen manufacturers. The thread form that Hydril created is now the lingua franca of premium tubing connections.

The PH6 competes not just with other proprietary designs, but with its own progeny—the dozen-plus look-alike connections charted above. In a market where operators can source PH6-type threads from Benoit, Tejas, Hunting, Precision Couplings, Atlas Bradford, TPS-Technitube, or a Chinese manufacturer, the original TenarisHydril PH6 trades on one thing above all: the name. And in OCTG, the name carries extraordinary weight.

Beyond the Blueprint

But the look-alike story is not simply one of duplication. In several measurable ways, the companies that adopted the PH6 thread form have pushed it beyond what Hydril originally engineered.

Better tool joints. Tejas Tubular offers nine-inch inertia-welded tool joints on TTS-6 Black—a genuine improvement over the upset-end joints on the original PH6. Inertia welding produces true full-face contact at the torque shoulder, yielding more accurate torque calculations, more reliable sealing, and more recutability over the life of the joint.

Application-specific variants. Benoit Premium Threading developed the BTS-6 as a heavy-wall variant with increased torque capacity, purpose-built for the punishing demands of milling and high-load workstrings in extended-reach laterals. Hunting's TS-HD refined the integral two-step for standard tubing weights across a broad size range. Each manufacturer addressed a different limitation of the original PH6 specification.

Hidden torque capacity. TenarisHydril publishes only make-up torques for PH6—never rotating, operating, or yield figures. Look-alike manufacturers performed formal torque-to-yield testing and proved what field hands long suspected: the connection handles substantially more torque than the published numbers suggest. Tejas now publishes a complete torque envelope for TTS-6 Black—data Hydril never provided. The original design had headroom its creators never advertised.

Unintended Applications

PH6 was designed as a production tubing connection—a permanent downhole fixture meant to hang in a well and hold pressure. It was not built for workovers. It was not built for frac plug drill-outs. It was not built for the punishing rotational abuse of milling operations in extended-reach laterals.

But operators pushed it there anyway. As horizontal wells grew longer and multi-stage frac completions became standard across the Permian, Eagle Ford, and Haynesville, someone had to mill out dozens of composite plugs per well—sometimes fifty or more in a single trip. The workstring needed a connection that could handle continuous rotation, high torque, and repeated make-and-break cycles without failing downhole. Operators reached for PH6, because it was what they already had on the rack, and it worked.

That it worked at all is a testament to the margin the original Hydril engineers built into their design. That it became the dominant drill-out connection across North America—in an application its designers never envisioned—is something closer to an accident of history. The two-step thread's deep stabbing made fast connections at the rig floor. The non-interference profile survived galling through dozens of trips. The metal-to-metal seal held under conditions no production tubing connection was expected to face. PH6 did not earn its place in the drill-out market by design. It earned it by not breaking.

The Next Generation

The drill-out market eventually attracted purpose-built alternatives. Tejas Tubular's Spearhead features a double-shoulder design whose torque capacity is independent of tool joint OD—meaning performance does not degrade as the joint wears. In 2-7/8" P-110, it carries a yield torque of 15,400 ft-lbs with 69% more wear life than PH6. Frac String Solutions' FSS-265, rated to 15,000 psi working pressure, takes a different approach with severe-service make-up torques reaching 7,200 ft-lbs in 2-7/8" S-135.

These connections are engineered for the specific abuse profile of milling frac plugs at 15,000 feet of measured depth. They are gaining traction with operators who run the numbers—the load envelopes, the wear curves, the total cost of ownership over hundreds of wells.

And yet.

PH6 and its look-alikes still dominate frac plug drill-outs across every major basin in North America. The rental yards are full of them. The rig crews know how to run them. The torque charts are taped to every doghouse wall. When a completion engineer specs a workstring, the default is still some variant of the two-step, six-pitch thread form that Hydril drew up more than forty years ago.

The next generation of workstring connections is here, and it is measurably better. But the PH6—a production tubing connection that was never designed for any of this—still will not give up the market. That is the most remarkable thing about this forty-year-old thread form: not that it works, but that nothing has replaced it.

Closing

The Thread That Outlived Its Patent

In most industries, a product that loses patent protection fades. Generics flood the market, the original brand becomes one of many, and market share fragments. The PH6 story is different.

The generics did come—a dozen or more of them. But the PH6 thread form did not fragment the market. It became the market. When a thread form is so dominant that every look-alike defines itself by compatibility with the original, the original has transcended its patent. It has become infrastructure.

Frank Seaver could not have known, in 1933, that his specialty company in Torrance would produce a connection still standard a century later—or that both “Hydril” and “PH6” would become generic names for their categories.

But he understood what still holds: in an industry where failure means blowouts and lives lost, the connection matters more than almost anything else going downhole. PH6 earned its place not through marketing or patent monopoly, but through four decades of gas-tight seals, trouble-free makeups, and reliable performance ten thousand feet below the surface.

That trust is why the thread endures. The patents expired. The company was sold. The founder is long gone. But every day, somewhere in the world, a rig hand stabs a pin into a box on a two-step, six-pitch thread and torques it to shoulder. And the connection holds.

Simplicity and quality.

— The Hydril Company philosophy, established 1933
