Chapter I

A Company Born in the Depression

In 1933, with the Great Depression tightening its grip on America, a man named Frank Roger Seaver made what must have seemed like a reckless bet. He took his life savings and purchased the struggling Doheny Stone Drill Company—a small outfit in Torrance, California with 50 to 75 employees, fighting for survival in an oil industry that was itself barely hanging on.

Seaver renamed the company Hydril—a combination of "hydraulic drilling equipment"—and made a pivotal decision. He dropped the company's general drilling equipment line to focus exclusively on specialty items. It was a bet-the-company move, and his employees, grateful to have jobs during uncertain times, threw themselves into making it work.

Within two years, they turned the company profitable.

Seaver operated as a "benevolent dictator who compartmentalized information and personally controlled all major decisions." His philosophy was built on two words: simplicity and quality.

— Encyclopedia.com, Hydril Company Profile

By the late 1930s, Hydril had produced the first hydraulically operated blowout preventer—a device so durable it actually hurt replacement sales. Seaver's solution was characteristically clever: he pivoted to a leasing model "to avoid saturating the market." In 1937, the company opened a manufacturing plant in Rochester, Pennsylvania. In 1940, Hydril Company of Texas was organized, producing military ordnance during World War II under the name "Texord."

Seaver had purchased far more land in Houston than seemed necessary for a manufacturing plant. Some considered it a waste of money. Decades later, it would be deemed an act of genius.

In 1946, an engineer named Granville Sloan Knox invented the spherical annular blowout preventer at Hydril. The annular BOP became so synonymous with the company that to this day, field hands often call any annular preventer simply "the Hydril"—the ultimate tribute to a brand that became the generic name for its category.

Chapter II

The PH6: A Thread That Changed Everything

PH stands for "Premium Hydril." The 6 means six threads per inch. It sounds unremarkable on paper. In practice, the PH6 became one of the most consequential pieces of engineering in the history of oil and gas.

The design that would become PH6 emerged from Hydril's pioneering work on two-step, non-interference thread profiles—a concept that was in commercial use by the mid-1970s. A 1976 patent (US 4,085,951) describes the "Hydril-type" connection as established technology, noting that "thousands of such units are discarded annually" at roughly $200 per unit. The PH6 was already an industry workhorse.

By the early 1980s, the PH6 designation was established as a commercial product. A 1985 European patent (EP 0,212,288A2) references the "Hydril PH-6 premium thread tubing connection" as existing prior art, confirming it was in widespread use before that date.

What Made It Revolutionary

The PH6's genius was its two-step, non-interference thread form. Threads are machined on two different diameters—two "steps"—providing extremely deep, stable stabbing. The non-interference design means the pin threads never contact the box threads during entry, which virtually eliminates the cross-threading that plagued earlier connections.

The seal system uses three independent metal-to-metal contact surfaces: a 30-degree reverse-angle external torque shoulder, a 90-degree intermediate shoulder, and a pin nose/box bore contact. No rubber. No elastomers. Just precision-machined steel sealing on steel, delivering gas-tight integrity at pressures exceeding 20,000 PSI.

The result was a connection that was fast to run, nearly impossible to cross-thread, gas-tight under extreme conditions, reusable across multiple make-and-break cycles, and proven in corrosive-resistant alloy (CRA) applications where galling is the constant enemy. It was, by every measure, a premium connection that earned its name.

Trouble-free make-up is routine with Hydril's two-step, non-interference thread style. The design combines the reusability of non-interference threads, metal seals, and substantial torque shoulders.

— TenarisHydril Running Manual, GDL23346

Chapter III

The CS: Compression Seal, Different Purpose

While PH6 conquered the workstring market, Hydril's engineers were building something else entirely. The CS—Compression Seal—was designed for a different set of demands: casing, completion strings, and the punishing world of macaroni tubing.

The CS was born from US Patent 4,521,042, filed July 5, 1983, by Hydril engineers Jan W. Blackburn and Burl E. Baron. The patent describes the innovation that would define the CS: two-stepped hooked threads with dissimilar-angled positive torque shoulders, creating a connection with dual torque shoulders—both an external and an intermediate—rather than PH6's single external shoulder arrangement.

This dual-shoulder design gives the CS up to 50% superior compression strength compared to single-shoulder connections. The three-point seal system—external, intermediate, and internal, all metal-to-metal—earned the CS a CAL IV gas-tight certification, the highest qualification level under ISO 13679.

Together, the PH6 and CS covered virtually every downhole application an operator could encounter. PH6 dominated workstrings and production tubing. CS owned casing, completions, and the small-diameter macaroni tubing market where few premium alternatives existed. Both shared the same running manual, the same field crews, and the same reputation for reliability that had been building since 1933.

The critical window: between 1995 and 2005, every foundational patent covering the Hydril two-step thread form, CS connection design, and wedge thread technology expired. The technology that had been protected for nearly two decades was now in the public domain.

Chapter V

The $2.16 Billion Bet

$2.16B
Tenaris Acquisition of Hydril • May 7, 2007

On February 12, 2007, Luxembourg-based steel giant Tenaris S.A. announced it would acquire Hydril Company at $97 per share—a 30% premium over the 30-day average stock price. The deal closed on May 7, 2007, for approximately $2.16 billion in equity value.

At the time of acquisition, Hydril was operating 13 manufacturing plants and 22 sales and service offices worldwide. The company had reported 2006 revenue of $503 million and net income of $91.3 million. It had been public on NASDAQ since 2000 under the ticker HYDL, though the Seaver family—now in its third generation of leadership under Christopher T. Seaver—had retained majority ownership.

The acquisition would enable the combined company to offer customers worldwide a full range of integral and coupled premium connection products for the most demanding applications.

— Paolo Rocca, Tenaris Chairman and CEO, February 2007

The premium connections business was retained and branded TenarisHydril. In April 2008, Tenaris sold Hydril's pressure control division to General Electric for approximately $1.115 billion—meaning Tenaris effectively paid just over $1 billion for the premium connections business alone. That pressure control division would eventually pass through GE, merge with Baker Hughes in 2017, and become part of HMH (Hydra-Rig, MHWirth, Hydril) in 2021.

Three generations of the Seaver family had built Hydril from a 50-person machine shop into a global brand. Now its legacy would live on under new ownership, and its connections—PH6, CS, and PH4—would be classified as the "Legacy Series" within the TenarisHydril portfolio.

Chapter VI

The Look-Alike Flood

When the foundational patents expired between 1995 and 2005, the door opened. And an entire industry walked through it.

The PH6 thread form had become so dominant—so deeply embedded in operator specifications, field crew muscle memory, and procurement databases—that it had achieved something rare in a proprietary design: it became a de facto industry standard. Not through a standards body. Not through an API committee. Through sheer market dominance.

When operators write "PH6-type" on a work order today, they might receive product from any of a dozen manufacturers. The thread form that Hydril created is now the lingua franca of premium tubing connections.

The look-alike ecosystem is not a story of counterfeiting. It is the natural consequence of patent expiration in a market where one design achieved total dominance. When operators spec "PH6-type," they are expressing a preference for the thread geometry—not necessarily the manufacturer. This creates price competition, supply diversity, and options for operators and distributors.

Chapter VII

The Legal Battles

Patent expiration did not mean the end of legal disputes. Hydril and later Tenaris fought to protect the intellectual property surrounding their connections, particularly the Wedge Thread technology that represented their next generation of innovation.

Hydril v. Grant Prideco (2003–2007)

The most significant legal battle involved Hydril's Wedge Thread technology. In 1994, Hydril had settled a lawsuit with XLS Holding regarding its wedge thread patent (US RE 34,467) and trade secrets. Under the settlement, XLS was licensed to use the technology only on large-diameter connections.

When Grant Prideco purchased XLS in 1997, Hydril gave written consent—but the merger agreement explicitly prohibited Grant Prideco from using the intellectual property in the small-diameter connection market. In January 2003, Grant Prideco's patent counsel sent letters to OMSCO, a Hydril-licensed drill pipe distributor, asserting that certain customer orders might violate Grant Prideco's patent.

The case reached the Federal Circuit in January 2007—just weeks before Tenaris announced the Hydril acquisition. The court reversed the dismissal of Hydril's antitrust and patent claims and remanded for further proceedings, finding that Grant Prideco's letters to Hydril's customers constituted sufficient enforcement activity to support a Walker Process antitrust claim.

Notably, the major litigation centered on the Wedge Thread technology—not PH6 or CS. By the time Hydril was aggressively litigating, the foundational PH6 and CS patents had already expired. The legal defense was focused on protecting the next generation of connections, not the Legacy Series that was already entering the public domain.

Chapter VIII

The Market Today

The premium connection market in 2026 is a fundamentally different landscape than the one Hydril dominated for half a century. But the PH6 thread form remains at its center.

TenarisHydril now organizes its connection portfolio into three tiers. The Legacy Series (PH6, PH4, CS) are described as "tried and proven performers which have provided many years of reliable service." The Wedge Series (Wedge 521, 523, 533, 563, 623) represents the next generation, with the Wedge 521 alone carrying 25 years of field history. The Blue Series is the latest innovation platform.

The "Legacy" designation does not mean obsolete. PH6 connections are still actively manufactured at TenarisHydril's premium threading facility at 201 Klein Street in Westwego, Louisiana—a facility employing approximately 2,285 workers across the Mississippi from New Orleans. The PH6 remains deeply entrenched in operator specifications worldwide.

The PH6 competes not just with other proprietary designs, but with its own progeny—the dozen-plus look-alike connections that are mechanically interchangeable. In a market where operators can source PH6-type threads from Benoit, Tejas, Hunting, Precision Couplings, Atlas Bradford, TPS-Technitube, or a Chinese manufacturer, the original TenarisHydril PH6 trades on one thing above all: the name. And in OCTG, the name carries extraordinary weight.

Closing

The Thread That Outlived Its Patent

In most industries, a product that loses patent protection fades. Generics flood the market, the original brand becomes one of many, and market share fragments. The PH6 story is different.

The generics did come—a dozen or more of them. But the PH6 thread form did not fragment the market. It became the market. When a thread form is so dominant that every look-alike defines itself by compatibility with the original, the original has transcended its patent. It has become infrastructure.

Frank Seaver could not have known, in 1933, that the specialty company he was building in Torrance would produce a connection design that would still be the industry standard a century later. He could not have anticipated that "Hydril" would become a generic term for blowout preventers, or that "PH6" would become shorthand for an entire category of premium thread.

But he understood something that still holds true: in an industry where failure means blowouts, environmental catastrophe, and human lives lost, the connection matters more than almost anything else going downhole. The PH6 earned its place not through marketing, not through patent monopoly, but through the accumulated trust of four decades of gas-tight seals, trouble-free makeups, and reliable performance ten thousand feet below the surface.

That trust is why the thread endures. The patents expired. The company was sold. The founder is 60 years gone. But every day, somewhere in the world, a rig hand stabs a pin into a box on a two-step, six-pitch thread and torques it to shoulder. And the connection holds.

Simplicity and quality.

— The Hydril Company philosophy, established 1933
